What is your company going to make? Please describe your product and what it does or will do.
Warscythe started from a pretty simple problem: finishing things never felt like anything. I’d finish something difficult and get a checkbox; meanwhile a game could make me grind for hours because there was always something on the other side. So I made an execution RPG where doing real things changes a persistent fantasy world. Finish your work, keep your habits, or train and you progress regions, collect artifacts, evolve Scythes, liberate Empresses, and kill dragons. Different people get attached to different parts of that loop, which is exactly the point.

Warscythe is for people who do hard things but don’t feel the weight of their own progress. I built it because I needed a system where effort, identity, discipline, and reward finally connected.

And there is no customizable avatar. You are the avatar. The things getting customized are your physique, your habits, and your willingness to show up every day.

Operations is where I put anything that actually needs to end-something as large as getting Warscythe into YC or as small as fixing a bug tonight. I set a deadline, break it into steps, and watch the completion percentage move as I work. But I hated how normal productivity apps make finishing something feel like the checkbox just disappears, so in Warscythe finishing changes the world: you recover artifacts, progress through regions, evolve your Scythe, and eventually fight the dragon holding that region. We’re also building Triumph Cards so finished work can become something worth sharing instead of another item buried in task history.

Rituals handles the opposite problem: things that shouldn’t end. Training, studying, medication, or whatever someone needs to keep doing. Those build streaks and permanent evidence of consistency instead of pretending every part of life is a one-off task.

Ledger is where none of that disappears when it’s over. It remembers what you finished, abandoned, earned, and maintained. I want someone who has used Warscythe for three years to look back and see a history of who they became, not 4,000 crossed-out checkboxes.

Fitness came from a very personal annoyance. Most fitness apps show progress through graphs and numbers. I find it much easier to project myself as a Greek god than stare at a spreadsheet and imagine what I’ll look like six months from now. I train almost every day, and it felt stupid that Warscythe could make finishing an Operation feel monumental while finishing a brutal workout basically gave me “workout completed.” So we’re rebuilding Fitness around the same idea as everything else: effort should leave evidence. Your training progresses your Deities, your PRs stay with you, and the Hall of Olympus becomes a museum of what your body has actually done. Later, I don’t even want you entering half of this manually. Your watch already knows you ran 8 km. Why should I ask you again? And if you want AI involved, it will then use your actual training history, goals, diet and calorie data, ask you the missing questions, and build research-backed plans around you instead of handing everyone the same generic routine. Before you walk into the gym, it will already have that day’s workout waiting for you. But it will stay optional -you can keep logging workouts forever without using AI once.

Integrations came from the same frustration. Your deadline is already in Gmail. Your meeting is already in Calendar. Your watch already knows you ran. Why am I asking you to open Warscythe and type all of that again? I don’t need Gmail to stop being Gmail. I just need Warscythe to know that something in Gmail requires you to do something by Friday.

Personalization matters to me because I make a lot of my own wallpapers and Warscythe art anyway, and I like my things looking like mine. So instead of giving people arbitrary CSS or a way to break the product, we’re moving toward controlled customization-safe things you can actually make yours, like Scythes, altars, backgrounds, and interface treatments.

Legion happened because my co-founder and I needed it ourselves. We wanted somewhere we could give each other work and actually know whether it got done. We looked around, couldn’t find what we wanted, so I came back and built Legion. We use it ourselves for SAT work and company stuff; even this YC application has been tracked there. If Abhay sleeps through his part of a deadline, I can cover his garrison. And every time I open Warscythe, the deadline is sitting there staring at me, which is mildly terrifying but useful. Later we want to take that further with Legion Wars, where groups compete through the work they’re already doing rather than through some separate game strapped on top.

And one fine morning I had the a thought staring at the war terminal: why can’t I eventually build Warscythe inside Warscythe? We already have the multiplayer execution logic underneath that idea-Legions for shared work, Operations that hold the objective, and realtime state syncing between people. The terminal-and-agent layer does not exist yet; that part is still architectural work.

An agent like Codex could attach to a Project Operation, get its own isolated session and only the MCP capabilities it needs, then stream what it’s reading, changing and testing through the same realtime layer the humans are already using. Commits, migrations and deployments can still stop for approval. Once that works for one agent, it can extend to several-frontend, backend, testing, security-working alongside the humans toward the same objective.

If we get that right, the end state is pretty funny: I want to be killing the dragon in one Warscythe region while using Warscythe to build the next one. A self-sustained system, which is conveniently also what SSS, the name of almost every Git commit I make, now stands for.

Idea
Why did you pick this idea to work on? Do you have domain expertise in this area? How do you know people need what you’re making?
I built Warscythe because nothing else worked for me; finishing difficult things used to feel like nothing. So I made something where it did. I tried Apple Reminders and Google Calendar to structure my routine and abandoned both within a minute - they were bland, and bland doesn't hold attention against everything else competing for it.

My domain expertise isn't credentialed in productivity software - it's in world-building and reward design. I'm a published poet, I write the world's lore myself. I've spent years obsessively dissecting why story-mode games like Palworld and Skyrim make objectively repetitive tasks - grinding, farming materials - feel worth doing: it's never the task itself, it's the visible progression and narrative weight attached to it. Warscythe applies that same mechanic to real execution.

That expertise is showing up in what users actually do, not just what we designed. Users started competing over who has more Epic-tier artifacts and completing real-world tasks specifically to earn them - a competition mechanic we never built, that emerged the moment artifacts became visible to friends. I've felt the same pull myself: I've caught myself doing a task partly to get the artifact, and felt genuinely disappointed seeing a friend with a rarer one than mine. That's one data point, not a proven pattern yet, but it's also why our fitness system is organized around classical Greek deities rather than muscle groups - a bet that status and story would outperform raw stats here too, which we're now watching play out rather than assuming.

I'm also the product's most demanding user - I run my SAT prep, this application, my publications, and my gym log entirely through it, so every gap in the system is one I hit myself first.
Who are your competitors? What do you understand about your business that they don’t?
Our closest competitors today are Habitica, Finch, and Forest. Broader competition includes AI productivity assistants like Littlebird, and over time, as Legion matures, we expect to compete with the default execution tools people use every day—Apple Reminders, Todoist, and Notion.

What we understand differently is that gamification is not an aesthetic layer. A lot of products add streaks, badges, cute characters, or fantasy art, but progress is still fundamentally communicated through lists, charts, timers, or dashboards. In Warscythe, progress is communicated through world-state: scythes, titles, deities, XP, coins, regions, dragon heads, fairy fragments, and liberated territory. The user should feel like they changed something, not just logged something.

We also think accountability should become multiplayer, not merely social. Habitica has parties, and tools like Reminders or Notion let people share and assign work. Our Legion system is aiming at something different: a persistent group can break a real objective into assigned sub-tasks with deadlines and priorities, and completion advances both the individual and the Legion. That turns collaboration from “we can all see the task list” into “the world moves only if each operative does their part.”

Our ambition is to win this market in three phases: first against superficial gamified trackers, then against the best motivation products, and eventually against the default tools people use to run real work. Their job is to help people remember, organize, or retrieve. Ours is to make people emotionally invested in executing at all.
How do or will you make money? How much could you make?
We make money without selling execution strength. So far we've made ₹400 from three cosmetic purchases-one ₹200 theme and two ₹100 scythes-without a monetization campaign.

Next we turn on ads for free users and an ad-free tier at ₹100/month in India and $3.60 internationally. We intentionally price below adjacent products, giving us room to raise prices later if retention and willingness-to-pay justify it.

The larger model compounds through several layers. Bundles raise basket size; the Saturday Night Market adds scarcity and a recurring reason to reopen the shop; seasonal Colosseum passes monetize prestige, cosmetics and new mythology without selling power. Profile visiting and artifact net worth make cosmetics socially visible, increasing their status value. Later, Divine Intervention adds paid AI fitness/meal planning, while advanced AI/MCP execution becomes another utility subscription.

We model this conservatively, using modest paid-conversion assumptions rather than assuming game-like whales or unusually high subscription uptake. On that basis, we estimate roughly $5–8k/month at 10k DAU and around $70–75k/month at 100k DAU (~$0.9M ARR), before stronger AI adoption, pricing increases, or a more international user mix.

Core execution stays accessible; we monetize identity, seasonality, convenience and advanced intelligence-not strength.
